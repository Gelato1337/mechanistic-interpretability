You are labelling sparse-autoencoder features of a language model. Each card describes ONE feature: a FACTS header of
computed numbers, the texts of a worked example with the tokens the feature fires on marked as [[token|activation]],
one more passage where it fires, and random fires from other prompts (language prompts and code prompts separately,
each with a few tokens of context). Newlines in tokens are shown as \n.

The question is: WHAT DETERMINES WHICH TOKENS IT FIRES ON. Answer with exactly one of five classes plus "other".
Go through the tests IN THIS ORDER and stop at the first that passes.

1. token     The "most frequent tokens" list is dominated by 1-3 exact strings that together make up roughly half or more
             of all fires, and the marked tokens in the texts are those strings (" the", "(", "def", " " before a number,
             "\n"). The strings are what matters, not where they are or what the passage is about.
2. word      The marked tokens are surface forms of ONE word or a small family of related words: inflections,
             capitalisation, translations into other languages, split-into-pieces spellings (" Samson", "Sa"+"mson",
             "Simson"). Test: different strings, same lemma.
3. position  The marked tokens have different meanings but sit in the same STRUCTURAL slot: first token of the passage,
             first token after a newline, after a punctuation mark, inside a bracket pair, every 2nd+ piece of a split
             word. Test: you could predict the fires from the layout alone, without reading the words. A punctuation
             mark itself is a token (test 1); the token AFTER it is a position.
4. concept   The marked tokens are many different words that share a MEANING or a GRAMMATICAL ROLE that the token itself
             carries: numbers, place names, pieces of personal names, function calls, negation, verbs of motion, loop
             keywords across programming languages, the verb that follows its subject, prepositions introducing a place.
             Test: you can name the category, predict new members, and the fires in the texts are the members only,
             not the tokens around them. GRAMMATICAL ROLES ARE CONCEPT, NOT CONTEXT.
5. context   The marked tokens are a MIXED BAG (nouns, verbs, function words, punctuation, subwords) with nothing in
             common as tokens, and the fires are dense inside passages of one kind: a topic, a language, a programming
             language, inside a code block, inside a quoted string or comment, formal register. Test: the passage decides,
             the token does not; typically the feature fires on a large share (roughly 15% or more) of the tokens of the
             passages it fires in, and a "topic" context feature fires in few prompts (under a few % of the corpus).
6. other     none of the tests passes with the evidence given.

Two traps that produce most wrong labels:
  * "fires inside code" is NOT context when the marked tokens are one string or one category (a space before a digit,
    operators, keywords): that is token or concept. Also check the random LANGUAGE fires: if the feature fires in
    ordinary prose prompts too, "code context" is wrong.
  * a feature that fires on the verb after a subject, on articles before a noun, on the object of a preposition, on the
    word after a name, is a grammatical-role feature: concept. Context is only for features where WHAT the token is
    does not matter at all.
Coverage numbers help: a feature on 1-7% of the tokens of a passage is almost never context; one on 20-70% usually is.
A feature marked <<ALWAYS-ON>> fires in most prompts of every kind; still classify it by what determines its fires
inside a text. Use the random fires to check that the pattern from the example holds elsewhere.

HARD RULES that override the impressions above (read the FACTS numbers before you read the texts):
  R1  CODE IS A DOMAIN, NOT A CONTEXT GRAIN. Half of the cards come from code prompts, so "fires inside code" is true of
      most of them and proves nothing. For a card whose fires are in code, ask WHICH tokens of the code fire. If you
      can name them as tokens or as a slot — statement-ending punctuation (';', ')', '}'), the first token of a line,
      operators, digits inside numbers, identifiers/variable names, keywords, the space before a digit — the answer is
      position / token / concept, never context. "inside code" is an acceptable grain ONLY when the fired tokens are of
      every kind at once (identifiers AND operators AND digits AND punctuation AND keywords) with none dominating, and
      the coverage is at least 15% of the tokens in code prompts.
  R2  COVERAGE GATE. Take the largest "% of tokens when it fires" among the member domains. Below 8%: context is not
      allowed unless the feature fires in under 3% of corpus prompts AND its fires are on unrelated words of one
      recognisable topic (then it is a topic-context feature). Between 8% and 15%: context needs the mixed bag to be
      really mixed (no token type you can name). Above 20%: context is the default unless test 1-4 clearly passes.
  R3  MULTILINGUAL IS NORMAL. Every prompt of this corpus exists in five languages, so a feature that "fires across
      English, Finnish, French, German, Swedish" is the usual case, not evidence for anything. Language is a context
      grain only when the feature fires in SOME languages and not in others (case j).
  R4  "diverse tokens" is not by itself evidence for context: position and concept features also fire on thousands of
      distinct strings. Diversity plus a nameable slot = position; diversity plus a nameable category = concept;
      diversity plus a topic or passage type AND high coverage = context.
  R5  NARRATIVE/PROSE IS ALSO A DOMAIN, NOT A GRAIN. "Narrative text", "general prose", "text passages" are not
      allowed grains. A prose feature is context only for a nameable topic (biblical narrative, geology, farms) or a
      register (inside a quotation, a comment, a list) or a language subset.
  R6  CLOSED STRING SETS ARE TOKEN. When the fired tokens are drawn from a small closed inventory of strings — the ten digits,
      single letters, punctuation marks and whitespace (' ', '\n', ';', ')', '}', ',', '.'), a handful of function words —
      the class is token even if many different strings occur and even if no single string reaches half. Test 1 passes
      when the "most frequent tokens" list is made of such short strings that together cover 40% or more of the fires.
      This holds in code too: a feature on ';', ')', '}', '\n' is token ("statement-ending punctuation"), never other.
      Do NOT answer other/low for these: "space, newline and punctuation" IS a describable token set.
      LIMIT: R6 holds only while the coverage is below 15%. A feature on 20% or more of the tokens of code prompts that
      fires on newlines AND spaces AND digits AND punctuation AND identifiers is the mixed bag of R1: context ("inside
      code"), because no closed set covers that much of a passage.
  R7  ONE LEMMA IS WORD, NOT CONCEPT. A feature on one word and its inflections and translations (France/French/français/
      Frankreich/franska; church/Kirche/kirkko/église; life/Leben/vie/liv; improve/improved/improving; whose/dont/jonka/vars) is word, even when that word names a category, a country, or a grammatical function. Concept needs
      MANY different lemmas of one kind (dozens of country names, dozens of verbs of motion). Count the lemmas: one or a
      handful of related lemmas = word; an open class = concept.
  R8  THE PUNCTUATION MARK IS TOKEN, THE TOKEN AFTER IT IS POSITION. A feature whose fires are ON newlines, spaces, periods,
      commas, semicolons or closing brackets is token (the strings themselves); position is only when the fires are on the
      varied tokens that FOLLOW such a mark or occupy a slot (first token of a line, token inside brackets).
  R9  "CONTINUES A WORD" IS A STATISTIC, NOT A SLOT. The FACTS line "% of fires are on a token that continues a word" is
      computed for every feature; a high value does not make the feature a position feature, and "tokens that continue a
      word" or "word-internal pieces" is NOT an acceptable token_type for position. Word-internal pieces are: context when
      they are dense in some languages and not others (case j) or dense in passages of one topic; word (test 2) when they
      are pieces of one word; concept when the pieces spell members of one category (place names, chemical names); and
      other/low when they are pieces of unrelated words with nothing in common. Position for pieces only in the narrow
      case "every 2nd+ piece of a split word, whatever the word, in every language" with under 10% coverage.
  R10 A SLOT NEVER COVERS 20% OF A PASSAGE. "First token after punctuation", "token following a keyword or bracket",
      "token after a name" describe at most one token in ten. If the coverage is 20% or more, the slot description is a
      rationalisation: the feature is context (name the passage kind: a language, a topic, inside code) or other.
  R11 If R1-R5 remove context and no other test passes, answer other with confidence low. Expect roughly one card in
      eight to be other/low; a run with almost no other/low answers is wrong.
